FE projectile on an RKPM slab
=============================

A steel projectile made of finite elements hits a concrete slab made of RKPM particles. The two bodies meet through
penalty contact: they touch, slide and separate, and the contact pushes back on the projectile. One explicit analysis
integrates both, EdelweissFE's ``NEDParallel``. This page walks through the model, piece by piece, and through what
must be checked before a result of such a run means anything.

The code on this page is that of example 191, ``examples/191_fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test``: the same model, small
(a quarter slab of 200 x 200 x 100 mm in 20 mm particles, the quarter projectile of two elements) and short (the first
40 us), which runs as a test and asserts the checks of "What to check" below. Run it with
``python -m pytest examples/191_fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test``, and change it to see a check fail. The production run
of "What the run shows" is the same model, larger and longer; its sizes are given there.

The model:

- **Projectile**: a steel cube of 50 mm, ``C3D20RUL`` (quadratic updated-Lagrangian hexahedra),
  ``VONMISES/HUGHES-WINGET``,
  300 m/s towards the slab, free.
- **Slab**: 100 mm thick, ``GradientEnhanced6FiniteStrainSQCNIxNSNI/3D/Hexa`` particles of GM7 at finite strains
  (``GM7/COROTATIONAL``), held at its edges.
- **Contact**: quadrature points on the faces of the slab particles near the impact against the faceted surface of
  the cube, through EdelweissFE's contact constraint (:mod:`edelweissfe.constraints.surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty`)
  with :class:`~edelweissmeshfree.constraints.base.contactpointsonparticlefaces.ContactPointsOnParticleFaces`.
- A **quarter model**, with the symmetry planes x = 0 and y = 0.

Units are N, mm, t and s throughout.

The projectile
--------------

The projectile is an ordinary finite element body, generated by EdelweissFE's box generator into the
:class:`~edelweissmeshfree.models.mpmmodel.MPMModel` (its node sets, element sets and surfaces are named after the
generator: ``projectile_left`` ... ``projectile_front``; ``back`` is the face at the smallest z, the nose):

.. literalinclude:: ../../../examples/191_fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test/fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test.py
    :language: python
    :pyobject: buildProjectile

Its faces that can touch the slab are tiled into flat contact facets by EdelweissFE's surface element generator
(:func:`edelweissfe.generators.surfaceelementgenerator.buildContactFacets`). The quadratic faces are triangulated
through their midside nodes: every facet is a flat triangle of three nodes of the element face, and the contact
passes its reaction at a point of a facet to these three nodes, with the weights of the point on the facet
(``facetConsistent``, the plain distribution; no facet carries weights of its own then). This is the second half of
``buildProjectile`` above.

The projectile gets its initial velocity at its nodes, in the entry ``V`` of the model's node field, after
``model.prepareYourself``: the nodes of finite elements always start from the model's velocity (see
:doc:`choosing_a_solver`, "Explicit dynamics"):

The slab
--------

The slab is a box of hexahedral particles with six nonlocal variables, the nonlocal strain of GM7 (field ``strain
symmetric``), on kernel functions matched to the particles; the material properties are those of ``GM7`` at the top
of the file:

.. literalinclude:: ../../../examples/191_fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test/fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test.py
    :language: python
    :pyobject: buildSlab

``GM7/COROTATIONAL`` drives GM7 with the increment of the logarithmic strain in the frame of the polar rotation and
rotates its stress back, so the stress of a rigidly rotating particle rotates with it. The last two properties are the
nonlocal viscosity :math:`\eta` and the micro-inertia of the nonlocal strain; with the micro-inertia
:math:`\eta^2/4` the nonlocal field is a critically damped wave, integrated by central differences on the particles'
own lumped inertia and damping. :math:`\eta` is the time the nonlocal strain lags behind the local one: it has to be
short against the rise of the contact force.

Supports and symmetry planes
----------------------------

The particles have no nodes on their boundary, so they are held by penalty constraints at points: the vertices of the
particles on a face.
:class:`~edelweissmeshfree.constraints.particlepenaltyweakdirichtlet.ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet` acts in an explicit
analysis as a support: it holds the given displacement components of its points at their initial positions. It does
so only for the displacement with every prescribed value zero (``{0: 0.0}``); ``NED`` refuses any other, since an
explicit analysis prescribes motion through velocities. The
vertices of a hexahedral particle on its faces are ``[0, 3, 4, 7]`` (x min), ``[1, 2, 5, 6]`` (x max), ``[0, 1, 4, 5]``
(y min) and ``[2, 3, 6, 7]`` (y max):

.. literalinclude:: ../../../examples/191_fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test/fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test.py
    :language: python
    :pyobject: holdTheSlab

A vertex moves with the particle's own deformation, its centre displacement and its smoothed deformation gradient:
by :math:`\sum_I G_I \Delta\boldsymbol{U}_I` in an increment, with the particle's shape functions linearized about its
centre :math:`G_I`
(:meth:`~edelweissmeshfree.particles.base.baseparticle.BaseParticle.getInterpolationVectorLinearizedAboutCenter`),
not with the shape functions at the vertex. With ``interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=True`` the support (and the
contact below, always) distributes its force with the same :math:`G_I`, the transpose of the vertex motion: its penalty
stiffness is symmetric and it does no work over a closed cycle of motion
(``edelweissmeshfree/constraints/test/vertexpenaltywork_test.py``). The default distributes the force with the shape
functions at the vertex, which is not conservative: it feeds energy into the particles at the supports, and a crushed,
soft particle there blows up, whatever the time increment. This model therefore opts in for every support.

Constrain the vertices, not the centres: the centres lie half a particle off the plane and move with Poisson's
effect. The support penalty must be stiff against the material next to it, or the plane gives way and springs back
(example 186), and below the limit :math:`2\sqrt{m/k}` of the time increment. The projectile's nodes on the symmetry
planes are held by ordinary Dirichlet conditions (``edelweissfe.stepactions.dirichlet``), which ``NED`` enforces.

The contact
-----------

.. literalinclude:: ../../../examples/191_fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test/fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test.py
    :language: python
    :pyobject: addTheContact

The contact points are four quadrature points on every face of the given particles (only those the projectile can
reach: every point within the search distance is searched in every increment), the faces inside the slab included,
which the projectile meets as it penetrates; the master is the faceted surface. It is the contact constraint that
couples two bodies of finite elements, with the particles' own contact points: each moves with its particle and
stands for its share of the face. The constraint pairs a point with a facet while the point approaches, pushes it
back out once it has entered -- never across a convex edge of the projectile, onto the side of the nose it entered
next to -- and lets it slide from facet to facet; a point that flows past the nose's edge, beside the projectile, is
released. At the edges, the gap is the distance to the edge (EdelweissFE's contact theory,
:ref:`edelweissfe:contact-corners`).
A point found inside without having been seen approaching gets no force and is reported. The force
on the particles and the reaction on the projectile's nodes are equal and opposite. See the documentation of the
constraint for the gap, the pairing and what it reports.

- **The penalty** is a stiffness per unit area, times the area a contact point stands for. The critical time step sees
  it: in every increment, the stiffness of the closed points is added to the frequencies of the nodes they couple, on
  the particles' side and on the projectile's (EdelweissFE's theory page on the critical time step, "Penalty
  constraints"). A penalty far stiffer than the concrete costs time increments, not stability.
- **The search distance** must exceed the distance a point travels towards the surface between two updates of the
  connectivity: a model with particles updates it in every increment (a model of finite elements alone only every
  ``contact-update-frequency`` increments). A point in contact is never lost to it. A warning in the journal says when
  points enter deeper than half of it.

The time increment
------------------

``NED`` takes the smaller of the critical time step of the elements (their own estimate, from an eigenvalue bound)
and the critical time step of the particles, estimated from their stiffness without assembling it, and scales it by
``courant-number``. The particles are estimated anew when the margin of their last estimate over the time increment in
use may be used up (see :doc:`choosing_a_solver`, "Explicit dynamics"): the larger the margin, the longer an estimate
lasts, and once the particles set the time increment, they are estimated in every increment. The estimate takes the
tangent of the gradient-enhanced particles as it is, unsymmetric: while the concrete under the nose is crushed, GM7's
stress depends on the nonlocal strain through the steep compressive boundary, far more strongly than the nonlocal
strain depends on the strain, and the bound follows the geometric mean of the two couplings, as the eigenvalues do.

In this model, the limit of the particles is that of the nonlocal field, set by its micro-inertia. It starts only a
little above the projectile's elements, so the elements set the time increment, and the margin of the particles is so
small that they are estimated in every increment from the start. The penalty springs of the contact and of the
supports add to the frequencies of the nodes they couple, in every increment: from the first touch on, the contact
springs at the nose set the time increment, a few percent below the limit of the projectile's elements and of the
particles. As the concrete under the nose is crushed, the particles' own estimate falls, at times below the
elements'. The tests of this page check this sequence. Check the result by halving the time increment.

Running it
----------

.. literalinclude:: ../../../examples/191_fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test/fe_projectile_on_rkpm_slab_test.py
    :language: python
    :pyobject: run

With ``output-frequency`` 1 the output managers see every increment: ``ImpactRecord`` (in the example) records the
contact forces of both sides, the points in contact and found inside, the deepest overlap, the projectile's velocity
and nose and the total kinetic energy in every increment. A long run adds a restart output manager, which writes
checkpoints, and resumes from one (:class:`~edelweissfe.utils.checkpoint.ResumeCheckpoint`, passed to
``step.solve``), so that it survives an interruption. Create the constraints, the supports included, before the
checkpoint is restored: a support holds the positions of the model it is created with. Nothing erodes failed concrete,
so the run must stop before the slab engulfs the projectile: then a slab particle ends up on the far side of a facet it
never approached, and the contact can no longer tell inside from outside. An output manager that raises ``ConditionalStop`` ends the step there.

What to check
-------------

A penalty contact in an explicit analysis can look healthy and be wrong: a time increment above the stability limit
of the contact springs, a penalty that decides the answer, or contact points that tunnel through the search distance
all produce plausible-looking histories. Record, in every increment, what the contact constraint reports and the
projectile's motion, and check:

- **Energy.** Nothing does work on the model (the supports and the symmetry planes hold still), so the total kinetic
  energy must never exceed the projectile's initial kinetic energy. A rebound is judged by its energy, not by the sign
  of the velocity: a penalty spring can push the projectile out only with energy the projectile brought in. ``NED``'s
  own energy table says that its balance is not usable in a run without external work and asks to watch the kinetic
  energy directly: this is that check.
- **Momentum.** ``contactForceOnSlave + contactForceOnMaster`` vanishes to round-off in every increment, and the
  projectile's momentum (its nodal masses times the nodal velocities) changes by the accumulated impulse of
  ``contactForceOnMaster``, the force of an increment times the mean of the increments before and after it (``NED``'s
  leapfrog), along the axis free of supports, to round-off.
- **Time increment.** Halving ``courant-number`` must change the penetration history by much less than the penalty
  does.
- **Penalty.** Over a tenfold range of the penalty, the penetration at a matched time may change by a few percent
  only, about the change of the overlap of the contact points. The force of the first touch is the penalty's (the flat
  nose meets a few points at full speed); its average over a few microseconds is not.
- **Search distance.** No point may be found inside (``numberOfPointsFoundInside`` stays zero), and the deepest overlap
  (``deepestPenetration``) stays far below the search distance.
- **Symmetry.** The quarter model reproduces the whole one: compare a small slab of the same particle size whole and
  quartered. The whole model is not exactly symmetric itself: the facets split every face along a fixed diagonal.

Example 191 asserts the checks of energy, action and reaction, time increment and search distance on its first 40 us.
Done on a small slab of the particle size of the production run, over its first 100 us, the checks came out as follows:
the kinetic energy never exceeded the initial one; action and reaction cancelled to round-off in every increment, and
the projectile's momentum followed the contact impulse to round-off; halving the time increment changed the penetration
by about a hundredth of a millimetre, a tenfold larger or smaller penalty by a few tenths of a millimetre, of the order
of the change of the overlap; the deepest overlap was about a tenth of the search distance and no point was found
inside; the quarter model followed the whole one to a few tenths of a millimetre. A run resumed from a checkpoint after
an interruption followed the uninterrupted one.

The critical time step of the particles costs a small share of an increment: the particles bound the row
sums of their absolute stiffness node by node, and only when the margin policy asks for it.

One finding matters more than any of these: the response depends on the particle size more than on the time
increment, the penalty or the symmetry. With GM7's internal length well below the particle size, the softening
concrete is resolved by the particles, not by the nonlocal strain.

What the run shows
------------------

A production run is the quarter of a slab of 600 x 600 x 100 mm in a few layers of particles and of the projectile in
2 x 2 x 4 elements, with the contact of this page (four points on every face of the particles near the impact), the
restart output manager and an output manager that stops the step at a chosen nose depth. The checks of "What to check"
hold over such a run: the kinetic energy never exceeds the initial one, action and reaction cancel to round-off, the
projectile's momentum follows the contact impulse to round-off, no point is found inside, and the deepest overlap stays
a small fraction of the search distance. The depths of the nose early in the run differ by a few percent between one
and four contact points per face, or with the contact on the vertices of the particles that the points on their faces
replaced.

Later quantities are not results of this model: when the nose reaches the stop depth, its velocity then and the force
late in the run change by tens of percent with the number of contact points per face. Once the concrete under the nose
is crushed, a handful of contact points carries the projectile, and their number decides how fast it is braked. A study
of the particle size and the contact points per face together has to come before such a number is quoted. When the
nose reached the stop depth, two fifths of the particles or more had cracked, through the whole thickness and out to
the supported edges, and the most crushed ones were under the nose and in the rear layer. The result depends on the
particle size (see above), which the regularization of GM7 does not remove at this size.
