FE projectile on an RKPM slab
=============================

A steel projectile made of finite elements hits a concrete slab made of RKPM particles. The two bodies meet through
penalty contact: they touch, slide and separate, and the contact pushes back on the projectile. One explicit analysis
integrates both, EdelweissFE's ``NEDParallel``. This page walks through the model, piece by piece, and through what
must be checked before a result of such a run means anything.

The model:

- **Projectile**: a steel cube of 50 mm, ``C3D20RUL`` (quadratic updated-Lagrangian hexahedra), ``VONMISES/HUGHES-WINGET``,
  300 m/s towards the slab, free.
- **Slab**: 1000 x 1000 x 100 mm, ``GradientEnhanced6FiniteStrainSQCNIxNSNI/3D/Hexa`` particles of GM7 at finite
  strains (``GM7/COROTATIONAL``), held at its edges.
- **Contact**: the vertices of the slab particles near the impact against the faceted surface of the cube,
  :class:`~edelweissmeshfree.constraints.explicit.particlepenaltyfacetcontactexplicit.ParticlePenaltyFacetContactExplicit`.
- A **quarter model**, with the symmetry planes x = 0 and y = 0.

Units are N, mm, t and s throughout.

The projectile
--------------

The projectile is an ordinary finite element body, generated by EdelweissFE's box generator into the
:class:`~edelweissmeshfree.models.mpmmodel.MPMModel` (its node sets, element sets and surfaces are named after the
generator: ``projectile_left`` ... ``projectile_front``; ``back`` is the face at the smallest z, the nose):

.. code-block:: python

    from edelweissfe.generators.boxgen import BoxgenSchema, Generator as BoxGenerator
    from edelweissfe.sections.solid import Section as SolidSection

    with model.topology.changes():
        BoxGenerator("projectile", model, journal, configuration=BoxgenSchema(
            x0=0.0, y0=0.0, z0=standoff, lX=25.0, lY=25.0, lZ=50.0, nX=2, nY=2, nZ=4, elType="C3D20RUL"))
    model.sections["projectile"] = SolidSection(
        "projectile", model, {"name": "VONMISES/HUGHES-WINGET", "properties": steel},
        [model.elementSets["projectile_all"]])

Its faces that can touch the slab are tiled into flat contact facets by EdelweissFE's surface element generator
(:func:`edelweissfe.generators.surfaceelementgenerator.buildContactFacets`). The quadratic faces are triangulated
through their midside nodes, and each facet carries the weights that pass a force at a point of the facet to the
nodes of the element face (``facetConsistent``); the contact uses them for the reaction on the projectile:

.. code-block:: python

    from edelweissfe.generators.surfaceelementgenerator import buildContactFacets

    facets = []
    with model.topology.changes():
        for face in ["back", "right", "top"]:  # the nose and the two outer faces of the quarter
            facetSet, _ = buildContactFacets(
                model, f"projectile_{face}", f"projectile_{face}_contact", "midside", "facetConsistent", journal)
            facets += list(model.elementSets[facetSet])

The projectile gets its initial velocity at its nodes, in the entry ``V`` of the model's node field, after
``model.prepareYourself``: the nodes of finite elements always start from the model's velocity (see
:doc:`choosing_a_solver`, "Explicit dynamics"):

.. code-block:: python

    displacement = model.nodeFields["displacement"]
    for node in model.nodeSets["projectile_all"]:
        displacement["V"][displacement.indexOfNode(node), 2] = -300e3

The slab
--------

The slab is a box of hexahedral particles with six nonlocal variables, the nonlocal strain of GM7 (field ``strain
symmetric``), on kernel functions matched to the particles:

.. code-block:: python

    material = {"material": "GM7/COROTATIONAL", "properties": gm7}  # ..., density, viscosity eta, micro-inertia

    def particleFactory(number, vertexCoordinates, volume):
        return MarmotParticleWrapper("GradientEnhanced6FiniteStrainSQCNIxNSNI/3D/Hexa", number, vertexCoordinates,
                                     0.0, approximation, material)

    generateBoxHexaParticleGrid(model, journal, particleFactory, name="slab", x0=0.0, y0=0.0, z0=-100.0,
                                l=500.0, h=500.0, t=100.0, nX=35, nY=35, nZ=7)
    generateKernelMatchingToParticle(model, journal, kernelFunctionFactory, model.particleSets["slab_all"],
                                     supportScalingFactor=2.4)

``GM7/COROTATIONAL`` drives GM7 with the increment of the logarithmic strain in the frame of the polar rotation and
rotates its stress back, so the stress of a rigidly rotating particle rotates with it. The last two properties are the
nonlocal viscosity :math:`\eta` and the micro-inertia of the nonlocal strain; with the micro-inertia
:math:`\eta^2/4` the nonlocal field is a critically damped wave, integrated by central differences on the particles'
own lumped inertia and damping. :math:`\eta` is the time the nonlocal strain lags behind the local one: it has to be
short against the rise of the contact force.

Supports and symmetry planes
----------------------------

The particles have no nodes on their boundary, so they are held by penalty constraints at points: the vertices of the
particles on a face.
:class:`~edelweissmeshfree.constraints.particlepenaltyweakdirichtlet.ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet` acts in an explicit
analysis as a support: it holds the given displacement components of its points at their initial positions. The
vertices of a hexahedral particle on its faces are ``[0, 3, 4, 7]`` (x min), ``[1, 2, 5, 6]`` (x max), ``[0, 1, 4, 5]``
(y min) and ``[2, 3, 6, 7]`` (y max):

.. code-block:: python

    # the symmetry plane x = 0: the normal component of the vertices on it
    model.constraints["symmetry_x"] = ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet(
        "symmetry_x", model, list(model.particleSets["slab_left"]), "displacement", {0: 0.0}, supportPenalty,
        constrain=[0, 3, 4, 7])
    # a supported edge: z and the normal component
    model.constraints["support_x"] = ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet(
        "support_x", model, list(model.particleSets["slab_right"]), "displacement", {0: 0.0, 2: 0.0},
        supportPenalty, constrain=[1, 2, 5, 6])

A vertex moves with the particle's own deformation, its centre displacement and its smoothed deformation gradient:
by :math:`\sum_I G_I \Delta\boldsymbol{U}_I` in an increment, with the particle's interpolation vector of an attached
point :math:`G_I` (:meth:`~edelweissmeshfree.particles.base.baseparticle.BaseParticle.getAttachedPointInterpolationVector`),
not with the shape functions at the vertex. The support (and the contact below) distributes its force with the same
:math:`G_I`, the transpose of the vertex motion: its penalty stiffness is symmetric and it does no work over a closed
cycle of motion (``edelweissmeshfree/constraints/test/vertexpenaltywork_test.py``). Distributing the force with the
shape functions at the vertex is not conservative: the production slab of this model blew up that way next to the
symmetry planes, once the concrete there had been crushed, whatever the time increment.

Constrain the vertices, not the centres: the centres lie half a particle off the plane and move with Poisson's
effect. The support penalty must be stiff against the material next to it, or the plane gives way and springs back
(example 186), and below the limit :math:`2\sqrt{m/k}` of the time increment. The projectile's nodes on the symmetry
planes are held by ordinary Dirichlet conditions (``edelweissfe.stepactions.dirichlet``), which ``NED`` enforces.

The contact
-----------

.. code-block:: python

    contact = ParticlePenaltyFacetContactExplicit(
        "contact", model, particlesNearTheImpact, facets, penaltyParameter, searchDistance, journal)
    model.constraints["contact"] = contact

The contact points are the vertices of the given particles (only those the projectile can reach: every point is
searched in every increment); the master is the faceted surface. The constraint pairs a point with a facet while the
point approaches, pushes it back out through that facet once it has entered, and lets it slide only onto the facets
next to it; a point found inside without having been seen outside gets no force and is reported. The force on the
particles and the reaction on the projectile's nodes are equal and opposite. See the class documentation for the
gap, the pairing and what the constraint reports.

- **The penalty** is a force per unit penetration of one contact point. It is a stiffness the critical time step does
  not see: keep :math:`2\sqrt{m/k}`, with :math:`m` the mass a contact point carries, well above the time increment.
- **The search distance** must exceed the distance a point travels towards the surface in one increment; a point in
  contact is never lost to it. A warning in the journal says when points enter deeper than half of it.

The time increment
------------------

``NED`` takes the smaller of the critical time step of the elements (their own eigenvalue bound) and the bound of the
stable time increment of the particles, bounded from their stiffness without assembling it, and scales it by
``courant-number``.
The bound of the particles is computed anew whenever a particle reaches other kernel functions (see
:doc:`choosing_a_solver`, "Explicit dynamics"); the contact pairs points anew in most increments, but that changes no
particle. It takes the tangent of the gradient-enhanced particles as
it is, unsymmetric: while the concrete under the nose is crushed, GM7's stress depends on the nonlocal strain through
the steep compressive boundary, far more strongly than the nonlocal strain depends on the strain, and the bound follows
the geometric mean of the two couplings, as the eigenvalues do. Here the limit of the particles is that of the nonlocal
field, set by its micro-inertia, and it stays above the projectile's elements: the elements set the time increment.
Neither bound sees the penalty stiffness of the contact or of the supports; check them by hand, and check the result by
halving the time increment.

Running it
----------

.. code-block:: python

    step = AdaptiveStepForExplicitSimulations(
        0, model, fieldOutputController, journal, jobInfo,
        NEDParallel(jobInfo, journal, **{"output-frequency": 1}),
        outputManagers, stepActions, stepLength=500e-6, startInc=1.0, maxInc=1.0, minInc=1e-12, maxNumInc=10**7)
    step.solve(resumeCheckpoint)

With ``output-frequency`` 1 the output managers see every increment: the run records the contact forces of both
sides, the momentum and kinetic energy of the projectile and the total kinetic energy in every increment. A restart
output manager writes checkpoints, and the run resumes from one (:class:`~edelweissfe.utils.checkpoint.ResumeCheckpoint`),
so a long run survives an interruption. Nothing erodes failed concrete, so the run must stop before the slab engulfs
the projectile: then a slab particle ends up on the far side of a facet it never approached, and the contact can no
longer tell inside from outside. An output manager that raises ``ConditionalStop`` ends the step there.

What to check
-------------

A penalty contact in an explicit analysis can look healthy and be wrong: a time increment above the stability limit
of the contact springs, a penalty that decides the answer, or contact points that tunnel through the search distance
all produce plausible-looking histories. Record, in every increment, what the contact constraint reports and the
projectile's motion, and check:

- **Energy.** Nothing does work on the model (the supports and the symmetry planes hold still), so the total kinetic
  energy must never exceed the projectile's initial kinetic energy. A rebound is judged by its energy, not by the sign
  of the velocity: a penalty spring can push the projectile out only with energy the projectile brought in.
- **Momentum.** ``contactForceOnParticles + contactForceOnMaster`` vanishes to round-off in every increment, and the
  projectile's momentum (its nodal masses times the nodal velocities) changes by the accumulated impulse of
  ``contactForceOnMaster``, the force of an increment times the mean of the increments before and after it (``NED``'s
  leapfrog), along the axis free of supports, to round-off.
- **Time increment.** Halving ``courant-number`` must change the penetration history by much less than the penalty
  does.
- **Penalty.** Over a tenfold range of the penalty, the penetration at a matched time may change by a few percent
  only, about the change of the overlap of the contact points. The force of the first touch is the penalty's (the flat
  nose meets a few points at full speed); its average over a few microseconds is not.
- **Search distance.** No point may be found inside (``numberOfPointsFoundInside`` stays zero), and the deepest overlap
  (``deepestPenetration``) stays far below the search distance.
- **Symmetry.** The quarter model reproduces the whole one: compare a small slab of the same particle size whole and
  quartered. The whole model is not exactly symmetric itself: the facets split every face along a fixed diagonal.

Done on a small slab of the same particle size, the checks came out as follows: the kinetic energy never exceeded the
initial one; action and reaction cancelled to round-off in every increment, and the projectile's momentum followed the
contact impulse to round-off; halving the time increment changed the penetration by a few hundredths of a millimetre,
a tenfold penalty range by about half a millimetre, the size of the change of the overlap; the deepest overlap was
about a tenth of the search distance and no point was found inside; the quarter model followed the whole one to a few
tenths of a millimetre. A run resumed from a checkpoint after an interruption followed the uninterrupted one to the
scatter of two runs: the runs are not bitwise reproducible, since threaded sums change the last digits, which reach the
time increment through the bound of the particles.

Two findings matter more than any of these. The bound of the particles' stable time increment was most of the cost of
an increment while it evaluated every particle's tangent; the particles now bound the row sums of their absolute
stiffness node by node, at a small share of an increment, and only when a particle reaches other kernel functions. And the
response depends on the particle size more than on the time increment, the penalty or the symmetry: with GM7's internal
length well below the particle size, the softening concrete is resolved by the particles, not by the nonlocal strain.

What the run shows
------------------

The quarter model of the 600 mm slab (3087 particles) ran until the nose reached the stop depth of 40 mm at
333 us, with the time increment set by the projectile's elements throughout. The checks held over the whole run: the
kinetic energy never exceeded the initial one, action and reaction cancelled to round-off, the projectile's momentum
followed the contact impulse to round-off, and no point was found inside. The projectile slowed from 300 to 148 m/s in
the first 100 us and to 57 m/s at the stop; the contact force averaged over 5 us peaked at 3.3 MN after 20 to 25 us
(the first touch, a few points at full speed, is a spike set by the penalty). At the stop, about two fifths of the
particles had cracked, through the whole thickness and out to the supported edges, and the most crushed ones were under
the nose and in the rear layer. The velocity history agrees with the August prototype of this model (a CDP slab, a
wider span), which reached 43.4 mm at 398 us; a bonded interface between projectile and slab stops at 34.4 mm. The
result depends on the particle size (see above), which the regularization of GM7 does not remove at this size.
