Choosing a solver

The nonlinear solver

An MPMModel is solved by EdelweissFE’s implicit static solvers, as a finite element model is: the solver asks the model what its equation system is made of (see EdelweissFE’s documentation, What the solver asks the model), and the model answers with its active domain.

Solver

Use it for

NIST

a quasi-static step; the elements, cells and particles are computed one after the other

NISTParallel

the same, with the elements, cells, particles and material points computed on all threads (OMP_NUM_THREADS)

NISTPArcLength

a quasi-static step under arc length control, e.g. past a limit point; the controller is a step action, named by the solver option arcLengthController

A simulation is set up from Python as any EdelweissFE step. The model holds what defines its connectivity – the managers that assign material points to cells (mpmManagers) and particles to kernel functions (particleManagers), the managers of the variationally consistent integration (vciManagers), and the constraints. The step holds the loads, the Dirichlet boundary conditions and the arc length controller, by type:

from edelweissfe.solvers.nonlinearimplicitstaticparallel import NISTParallel
from edelweissfe.steps.adaptivestep import AdaptiveStep
from edelweissfe.steps.stepmanager import StepActionCollection

from edelweissmeshfree.solvers.meshfreeconvergencecriterion import (
    jobInfoWithMeshfreeTolerances,
    meshfreeCriterionOptions,
)

model.particleManagers.append(particleManager)
model.vciManagers.append(vciManager)
for constraint in constraints:
    model.constraints[constraint.name] = constraint

stepActions = StepActionCollection()
stepActions["distributedload"][pressure.name] = pressure

jobInfo = jobInfoWithMeshfreeTolerances()
solver = NISTParallel(jobInfo, journal, linsolver="pardiso", **meshfreeCriterionOptions)
step = AdaptiveStep(
    0, model, fieldOutputController, journal, jobInfo, solver, outputManagers, stepActions,
    startInc=0.1, maxInc=0.1, minInc=1e-3, maxNumInc=100, maxIter=15, criticalIter=5, maxGrowIter=10,
)
step.solve()

jobInfoWithMeshfreeTolerances and meshfreeCriterionOptions set the convergence criterion of EdelweissMeshfree’s former solvers (see meshfreeconvergencecriterion); tolerances that differ are passed by name, e.g. jobInfoWithMeshfreeTolerances(fluxResidualTolerance=5e-3). The settings of the former solvers map onto EdelweissFE’s as follows:

Restart. EdelweissFE’s restart output manager writes a checkpoint of the converged state; an MPMModel adds the state of its material points and particles, from which their positions follow (see writeRestart()), and the state of the output managers of the step. A resumed run builds the model as the checkpointed run did, restores its state, and continues the step:

from edelweissfe.outputmanagers.restart import OutputManager as RestartOutputManager
from edelweissfe.outputmanagers.restart import RestartOutputManagerSchema
from edelweissfe.utils.checkpoint import ResumeCheckpoint

restartOutput = RestartOutputManager(
    "restart", model, fieldOutputController, journal, None,
    configuration=RestartOutputManagerSchema(writeInterval=1, baseName="restart"),
)
outputManagers = [ensightOutput, restartOutput]

# in the resumed run, right after model.prepareYourself(journal):
resumeCheckpoint = ResumeCheckpoint("restart_1.h5")
resumeCheckpoint.restoreModel(model, journal)
# ... set up the step as before, then
step.solve(resumeCheckpoint)

Differences to the former solvers. The same settings give the same Newton iterations; a few things are different by design:

  • The reaction forces P at the nodes are those of the elements, cells and particles; the former solvers added the forces of the constraints.

  • A constraint that couples the same degree of freedom more than once adds every contribution to it; the former solvers kept only one of them.

  • A cell, particle or material point whose material fails in Marmot asks for the increment to be cut back to half its size, as an EdelweissFE element does, and the step cuts back by the larger of this and cutbackFactor; the former solvers always cut back by their own factor.

  • A step that reaches maxNumInc ends there, and the simulation continues; the former solvers reported a failed incrementation. A step that cannot reduce its increment any further raises StepFailed.

  • The quadratic extrapolation is the parabola through the last three accepted states; the former quadratic predictor was not exact.

  • The scalar variables, e.g. Lagrange multipliers, are checked for convergence with EdelweissFE’s tolerances.

The former solvers are kept, deprecated, in edelweissmeshfree.solvers.deprecated.

Choosing a linear solver

Every Newton iteration of an implicit meshfree simulation spends most of its time in one linear solve, so the choice of linear solver and its settings usually matters more than anything else you can tune. This section collects what was learned on RKPM problems – what helps, what turned out not to matter – and how to find out for your own model.

Note

The advice was gathered on one problem class: an impact problem discretized with RKPM particles of a large support, on deformed states with active penalty contact. The support radius changes the behaviour of the linear solvers more than anything else (see the operator), so treat the advice as a hypothesis to check on a different discretization.

Start here

A first choice

Situation

Use

Why

small and medium models

pardiso with reuseSymbolicFactorization=True

a direct factorization is fastest while its fill-in stays moderate, and the symbolic reuse saves most of its setup

large models

blockamg

the iteration count of AMG stays nearly constant as the model grows; the cost of a direct factorization grows faster than the model

models whose staging assembly exceeds its pair counter

blockamg, and systemMatrixAssembly="direct" on the solver

the staging assembly cannot index more than INT32_MAX contributions (see System matrix assembly)

memory bound rather than time bound

blockamg + systemMatrixAssembly="direct"

the fill-in of a direct factorization is the one memory term that is not bounded by the model size

Where the crossover between a direct and an iterative solver lies depends on the discretization: measure both on a representative state of your model.

The direct solver

reuseSymbolicFactorization

Turn this on for a Newton sequence. The sparsity pattern is the same in all iterations of an increment, so the reordering and the symbolic factorization are computed once instead of in every solve. The solutions agree to round-off.

Threading

The direct solve stops scaling well beyond a moderate number of threads. Do not size a machine for a direct solver on its thread count alone.

The operator is the thing

Before tuning a preconditioner, know what the RKPM operator looks like, because it does not resemble the sparse operators AMG was designed for:

  • Structurally dense: every row has as many stored entries as there are kernel functions in reach of the particles around its node – with a large support, a large multiple of what a finite element mesh gives.

  • Numerically sparse: the typical stored off-diagonal entry is orders of magnitude smaller than the diagonal; only a few entries per row carry weight.

  • Symmetric to round-off, with a mild spread of the diagonal.

Two consequences follow, and both are counter-intuitive.

Penalty contact is not the problem. It does not create the scale separation that would confuse a strength-of-connection criterion; the diagonal spread stays mild.

The operator is numerically far sparser than it is structurally. Smoothed aggregation weighs every stored entry where a handful matter, and the Galerkin product carries them all down to the coarse levels. This is the most useful thing to know about tuning AMG here, and it is what dropTolerance exploits.

The support radius dominates. A larger support costs many more outer iterations with the same preconditioner. If the iteration counts are unacceptable, the discretization is a bigger lever than the solver – but the support radius is an accuracy decision, not a free one: the solution changes with it.

Settings that matter

dropTolerance – the largest single win

Builds each AMG hierarchy from a copy of the matrix without the off-diagonal entries \(a_{ij}\) with \(|a_{ij}| < \tau \sqrt{|a_{ii}| |a_{jj}|}\), \(\tau\) = dropTolerance. Only the preconditioner is sparsified: the operator the Krylov method applies, and therefore the residual it drives to zero, is untouched. A change of the preconditioner cannot move the converged solution beyond the outer tolerance – it can only change how many iterations reaching it takes.

On the RKPM operator, the outer iterations per solve stay flat over orders of magnitude of dropTolerance, while the hierarchy keeps only a small fraction of the entries. The whole gain is a cheaper iteration, not a faster convergence.

The default is 0.0 (off): the point where the quality of the preconditioner breaks down was never reached, so the safe end of the range is unknown, and it was explored on one operator only. Start in the middle of the range that was tried, e.g. 1e-4, and check the iteration counts per solve on your model.

Use plain truncation. Adding the dropped entries to the diagonal (lumpDroppedEntries), so that the row sums are kept exactly, buys nothing on this operator and costs an extra pass over the matrix. Together with the rigid body modes and the strength-of-connection threshold having no effect (below), this says that near-null-space and strength-of-connection fidelity are not what limits this preconditioner; the cost of applying a hierarchy built from a numerically dense operator is, and the truncation removes it.

The criterion is scale invariant and symmetric in \((i, j)\), so the sparsified block stays symmetric, as smoothed aggregation and the Chebyshev smoother require. The diagonal is always kept, so no row can be emptied.

symmetric

Set symmetric=False for a single-field problem. With one field the reverse block sweep is applied to identical input and returns an identical result, so it is a wasted AMG cycle per outer iteration.

tolerance versus adaptive forcing

This one has a trap. tolerance is unset by default, which selects Eisenstat–Walker forcing. Passing a number for tolerance switches forcing off entirely: the tolerance is then the same in every solve.

Forcing is not a reliable win here. Looser linear solves early in the Newton loop buy fewer inner iterations, and can pay for them with more Newton iterations; depending on the model size, forcing came out ahead or clearly behind a fixed tolerance. Measure it on your problem before relying on it.

Settings that did nothing

These were each tested against the configuration above, and are recorded so that time is not spent on them again:

  • The rigid body modes as near-null-space (rigidBodyModesPerAggregate): results identical to the last digit; inert on this operator.

  • More block sweeps (sweeps=2): identical results at a large extra cost. For one field a further sweep applies the same AMG to the same input.

  • The strength-of-connection threshold: changes the results, but not the iteration counts in any consistent direction.

  • Refreshing the hierarchy eagerly instead of reusing it (reusePreconditioner): the iteration counts barely change, and the time grows. Reused and refreshed solves need about the same number of iterations.

  • Lumping the dropped entries (lumpDroppedEntries): see above; it keeps the row sums exactly, and changes nothing else but the cost of the build.

Where the time goes

Depending on the support radius, the Krylov iteration is only a part of the linear solve. With low iteration counts the equilibration of the matrix, the build of the hierarchy and the split into fields can together cost more than the outer iterations. The split is skipped for a single field; the equilibration is computed in every solve.

If your iteration counts are low, do not assume the Krylov loop is where your time is. Read the blockamg entries of the performance table before optimizing anything.

Memory, and what limits the model size

For large models the binding constraint is usually memory, and usually a transient rather than the steady state:

  • Use systemMatrixAssembly="direct" once the staging assembly approaches its pair counter (see System matrix assembly). Assembly cost and memory then grow linearly with the model.

  • The peak memory is set by setup transients – the construction of the CSR pattern, and its reconstruction when the connectivity changes – not by the assembly or the solve. Size a machine for the peak, and expect it to lie well above what the solve itself occupies.

Diagnosing a slow solve

Read the per-solve log lines. blockamg prints one line per solve with its outer iteration count, whether the hierarchy was refreshed or reused, the tolerance it was given, and the time taken.

Pair each solve with the residual it was asked to reduce. The iteration counts vary strongly within an increment, from few early to many in the last Newton iterations, so a mean over an increment hides the structure. An early attempt to characterize AMG on large models concluded that it degraded with the model size, when in fact the expensive solves were the late Newton ones, present in the same proportion at every size: the curves of iterations against residual of small and large models lie on top of each other.

Pitfalls when measuring

Divide iteration totals by the number of solves. Two configurations rarely take the same number of Newton iterations, so summed outer iteration counts are not comparable.

Establish a noise floor, and match it to what you vary. Two runs of the same build differ slightly in iteration counts; two builds of nominally equivalent code differ more. A floor measured by rerunning one build does not license conclusions about a code change, and neither floor is the right comparator for a preconditioner change, where the Newton path necessarily differs.

A preconditioner change cannot be validated by comparing solutions. It changes the path of the iterates by construction. What must hold is that every increment still meets the same convergence criterion, and that the number of cutbacks does not grow.

Do not trust a share measured in another regime. The share of a component of the linear solve depends on how much of the total the Krylov loop takes, which changes completely with the support radius.

A worked configuration

For an RKPM problem above the crossover, on the deformed states where the work actually happens:

from edelweissfe.config import registry
from edelweissfe.config.linsolve import LinearSolverDefinition
from edelweissfe.utils.schema import buildSchemaFromOptions

blockamg, blockamgSchema = registry.lookup("linsolver", "blockamg")
jobInfo["linearSolvers"] = {
    "amg": LinearSolverDefinition(
        blockamg,
        buildSchemaFromOptions(
            blockamgSchema,
            {
                "tolerance": 1e-4,  # fixed; note this switches Eisenstat--Walker forcing off
                "symmetric": False,  # the reverse sweep is a wasted cycle for a single field
                "dropTolerance": 1e-4,  # cheaper iterations; verify on your own operator first
            },
        ),
    )
}

solver = NISTParallel(
    jobInfo,
    journal,
    linsolver="amg",
    **meshfreeCriterionOptions | {"systemMatrixAssembly": "direct"},
)

Then check three things in the performance table before tuning further: the share of the linear solve that is actually the outer Krylov iteration, the outer iteration counts in the late Newton iterations of an increment, and whether the peak memory is set by a setup transient rather than by the solve.
