Solvers

EdelweissFE’s implicit solvers

An MPMModel answers what EdelweissFE’s implicit solvers ask a model (see the models), so NIST, NISTParallel and NISTPArcLength solve it as they solve a finite element model, built from Python as any EdelweissFE step; see Choosing a solver. Their convergence criterion with the settings of EdelweissMeshfree’s former solvers is a parameter set of EdelweissFE’s legacy criterion:

The convergence criterion of EdelweissMeshfree’s (deprecated) implicit solvers, as a parameter set of EdelweissFE’s legacy criterion (see edelweissfe.solvers.base.convergencecriteria), for EdelweissFE’s implicit solvers NIST, NISTParallel and NISTPArcLength, which solve an MPMModel as they solve a finite element model.

A solver built from Python takes the tolerances per field from its jobInfo and the remaining parameters as options:

jobInfo = jobInfoWithMeshfreeTolerances()
solver = NISTParallel(jobInfo, journal, linsolver="pardiso", **meshfreeCriterionOptions)

The explicit solver

Explicit dynamics of a meshfree (RKPM) model: central differences on a discretization that moves with its particles.

The scheme is the leapfrog of EdelweissFE’s NED: the velocity is staggered half an increment behind the displacement, and the acceleration is the net force \(\boldsymbol{f}\) (external minus internal, with the loads and constraints) divided by a lumped mass,

\[\boldsymbol{v}_{n+1/2} = \boldsymbol{v}_{n-1/2} + \tfrac{1}{2} \left( \Delta t_{n+1} + \Delta t_n \right) \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} \boldsymbol{f}_n, \qquad \Delta \boldsymbol{u}_{n+1} = \Delta t_{n+1}\, \boldsymbol{v}_{n+1/2},\]

with \(\Delta t_0 = 0\) at the start of a step: the first increment of motion of a step is the leapfrog’s half step from the velocity at its start, \(\boldsymbol{v}_{1/2} = \boldsymbol{v}_0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \Delta t_1 \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} \boldsymbol{f}_0\).

What differs is the discretization it is applied to. NED integrates nodes that carry their total solution on a fixed mesh, and an element advances its state and returns its force in one call. The kernel functions of an RKPM model move with its particles, and the state lives in the particles (updated Lagrangian), so one increment has three parts:

  1. advance: every particle takes the increment \(\Delta \boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\) on the kernel functions it was assigned at \(t_n\) (updatePhysicsExplicit), and moves;

  2. reconnect: the model assigns the particles to the kernel functions they reach now (updateConnectivity()), and the discrete system follows;

  3. evaluate: on the new kernel functions, the particles return their internal force, their lumped mass and their lumped momentum, and the loads and constraints their forces, at the configuration just accepted.

The lumped mass is therefore assembled anew in every increment, and the nodal values are coefficients of the current kernel functions. The velocity is the state the next increment starts from, so the model owns it, as it owns the velocity of a finite element model (see EdelweissFE’s documentation page Node field results): the velocity of every node of the kernel functions is written to the entry V of the node fields at acceptance, and read back by node whenever the discrete system is rebuilt. A node keeps its velocity while the active nodes change around it, and a node whose kernel function has just become active starts at rest. If the option projectVelocityEveryIncrement says so, the velocity is instead projected from the particles’ momentum in every increment, \(\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} \boldsymbol{p}\).

At acceptance, the results reach the model in two parts, each through the discrete system that holds them: the increment dU (and the velocity it was taken with) on the kernel functions of its start, before reconnecting; the force P, the velocity the next increment starts from and the acceleration on the kernel functions of its end. V is the staggered velocity \(\boldsymbol{v}_{n+1/2}\), as in NED. A is the acceleration of the scheme, \(\boldsymbol{a}_n = \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} \boldsymbol{f}_n\) (zero at a node without mass), and P the internal force with the inertial force, \(\boldsymbol{f}_\mathrm{int} + \boldsymbol{M} \boldsymbol{a}_n\): at a node with mass, this is the force of the loads and constraints. The solution U of the kernel functions’ nodes is zero: they carry no total solution.

A step hands the next one the velocity and the acceleration at its end time, as in NED: at the end of the step, the velocity is advanced by the half increment it lags behind (advanceVelocity()). The time increment is the step’s: particles report no critical time step. The first increment of a step must be the zero increment, which assembles the lumped mass of the configuration the step starts from. Where the step takes the motion from, the model says (carriersOfTheMotion()):

  • If the model holds the motion at its nodes – after a step of this solver, or of NID integrating the inertia at the nodes – the step continues it as NED does. It starts from the velocity of the nodes, carried by node as in every rebuild, and from the net force of their acceleration, \(\boldsymbol{f} = \boldsymbol{M} \boldsymbol{a}\). The force evaluated at the zero increment would not do: the particles have taken a zero-length increment, and the stabilization of their integration refers to the increment taken last.

  • Otherwise – at the start of an analysis, with the initial velocity given to the particles, after a quasi-static step, or after NID integrating the inertia in the particles – it evaluates the forces of the configuration and projects the velocity from the particles’ momentum.

The first increment of motion is then the leapfrog’s half step from that velocity. After a step of this solver, the leapfrog continues as though the two steps were one. A resumed step continues from the velocity of the model and the net force and inverse mass of its checkpoint instead.

Deprecated implicit solvers

The implicit solvers EdelweissMeshfree had before EdelweissFE’s implicit solvers solved its models.

Deprecated since version An: MPMModel is solved by EdelweissFE’s implicit static solvers NIST, NISTParallel and NISTPArcLength; see the documentation page Choosing a solver.

This package keeps the old solvers, so that the examples which still use them keep working:

  • NonlinearQuasistaticSolver (replaced by NIST and NISTParallel),

  • NonlinearQuasistaticMarmotArcLengthSolver (replaced by NISTPArcLength),

  • their common bases implicitbase and nonlinearsolverbase, and the predictors they take (predictors; EdelweissFE’s solvers have the option extrapolation).

They are frozen: they get no new features, only the adaptations that later changes of the interfaces they call force. Constructing one emits a DeprecationWarning that names its replacement. The package is deleted once no example needs it any more.
