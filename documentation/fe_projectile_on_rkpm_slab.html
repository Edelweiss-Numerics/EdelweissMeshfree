FE projectile on an RKPM slab

A steel projectile made of finite elements hits a concrete slab made of RKPM particles. The two bodies meet through penalty contact: they touch, slide and separate, and the contact pushes back on the projectile. One explicit analysis integrates both, EdelweissFE’s NEDParallel. This page walks through the model, piece by piece, and through what must be checked before a result of such a run means anything.

The model:

  • Projectile: a steel cube of 50 mm, C3D20RUL (quadratic updated-Lagrangian hexahedra), VONMISES/HUGHES-WINGET, 300 m/s towards the slab, free.

  • Slab: 600 x 600 x 100 mm, GradientEnhanced6FiniteStrainSQCNIxNSNI/3D/Hexa particles of GM7 at finite strains (GM7/COROTATIONAL), held at its edges.

  • Contact: the vertices of the slab particles near the impact against the faceted surface of the cube, ParticlePenaltyFacetContactExplicit.

  • A quarter model, with the symmetry planes x = 0 and y = 0.

Units are N, mm, t and s throughout.

The projectile

The projectile is an ordinary finite element body, generated by EdelweissFE’s box generator into the MPMModel (its node sets, element sets and surfaces are named after the generator: projectile_left … projectile_front; back is the face at the smallest z, the nose):

from edelweissfe.generators.boxgen import BoxgenSchema, Generator as BoxGenerator
from edelweissfe.sections.solid import Section as SolidSection

with model.topology.changes():
    BoxGenerator("projectile", model, journal, configuration=BoxgenSchema(
        x0=0.0, y0=0.0, z0=standoff, lX=25.0, lY=25.0, lZ=50.0, nX=2, nY=2, nZ=4, elType="C3D20RUL"))
model.sections["projectile"] = SolidSection(
    "projectile", model, {"name": "VONMISES/HUGHES-WINGET", "properties": steel},
    [model.elementSets["projectile_all"]])

Its faces that can touch the slab are tiled into flat contact facets by EdelweissFE’s surface element generator (edelweissfe.generators.surfaceelementgenerator.buildContactFacets()). The quadratic faces are triangulated through their midside nodes: every facet is a flat triangle of three nodes of the element face, and the contact passes its reaction at a point of a facet to these three nodes, with the weights of the point on the facet (facetConsistent, the plain distribution; no facet carries weights of its own then):

from edelweissfe.generators.surfaceelementgenerator import buildContactFacets

facets = []
with model.topology.changes():
    for face in ["back", "right", "top"]:  # the nose and the two outer faces of the quarter
        facetSet, _ = buildContactFacets(
            model, f"projectile_{face}", f"projectile_{face}_contact", "midside", "facetConsistent", journal)
        facets += list(model.elementSets[facetSet])

The projectile gets its initial velocity at its nodes, in the entry V of the model’s node field, after model.prepareYourself: the nodes of finite elements always start from the model’s velocity (see Choosing a solver, “Explicit dynamics”):

displacement = model.nodeFields["displacement"]
for node in model.nodeSets["projectile_all"]:
    displacement["V"][displacement.indexOfNode(node), 2] = -300e3

The slab

The slab is a box of hexahedral particles with six nonlocal variables, the nonlocal strain of GM7 (field strain symmetric), on kernel functions matched to the particles:

material = {"material": "GM7/COROTATIONAL", "properties": gm7}  # ..., density, viscosity eta, micro-inertia

def particleFactory(number, vertexCoordinates, volume):
    return MarmotParticleWrapper("GradientEnhanced6FiniteStrainSQCNIxNSNI/3D/Hexa", number, vertexCoordinates,
                                 0.0, approximation, material)

generateBoxHexaParticleGrid(model, journal, particleFactory, name="slab", x0=0.0, y0=0.0, z0=-100.0,
                            l=300.0, h=300.0, t=100.0, nX=21, nY=21, nZ=7)
generateKernelMatchingToParticle(model, journal, kernelFunctionFactory, model.particleSets["slab_all"],
                                 supportScalingFactor=2.4)

GM7/COROTATIONAL drives GM7 with the increment of the logarithmic strain in the frame of the polar rotation and rotates its stress back, so the stress of a rigidly rotating particle rotates with it. The last two properties are the nonlocal viscosity \(\eta\) and the micro-inertia of the nonlocal strain; with the micro-inertia \(\eta^2/4\) the nonlocal field is a critically damped wave, integrated by central differences on the particles’ own lumped inertia and damping. \(\eta\) is the time the nonlocal strain lags behind the local one: it has to be short against the rise of the contact force.

Supports and symmetry planes

The particles have no nodes on their boundary, so they are held by penalty constraints at points: the vertices of the particles on a face. ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet acts in an explicit analysis as a support: it holds the given displacement components of its points at their initial positions. It does so only for the displacement with every prescribed value zero ({0: 0.0}); NED refuses any other, since an explicit analysis prescribes motion through velocities. The vertices of a hexahedral particle on its faces are [0, 3, 4, 7] (x min), [1, 2, 5, 6] (x max), [0, 1, 4, 5] (y min) and [2, 3, 6, 7] (y max):

# the symmetry plane x = 0: the normal component of the vertices on it
model.constraints["symmetry_x"] = ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet(
    "symmetry_x", model, list(model.particleSets["slab_left"]), "displacement", {0: 0.0}, supportPenalty,
    constrain=[0, 3, 4, 7], interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=True)
# a supported edge: z and the normal component
model.constraints["support_x"] = ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet(
    "support_x", model, list(model.particleSets["slab_right"]), "displacement", {0: 0.0, 2: 0.0},
    supportPenalty, constrain=[1, 2, 5, 6], interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=True)

A vertex moves with the particle’s own deformation, its centre displacement and its smoothed deformation gradient: by \(\sum_I G_I \Delta\boldsymbol{U}_I\) in an increment, with the particle’s shape functions linearized about its centre \(G_I\) (getInterpolationVectorLinearizedAboutCenter()), not with the shape functions at the vertex. With interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=True the support (and the contact below) distributes its force with the same \(G_I\), the transpose of the vertex motion: its penalty stiffness is symmetric and it does no work over a closed cycle of motion (edelweissmeshfree/constraints/test/vertexpenaltywork_test.py). The default distributes the force with the shape functions at the vertex, which is not conservative: it feeds energy into the particles at the supports, and a crushed, soft particle there blows up, whatever the time increment. This model therefore opts in everywhere.

Constrain the vertices, not the centres: the centres lie half a particle off the plane and move with Poisson’s effect. The support penalty must be stiff against the material next to it, or the plane gives way and springs back (example 186), and below the limit \(2\sqrt{m/k}\) of the time increment. The projectile’s nodes on the symmetry planes are held by ordinary Dirichlet conditions (edelweissfe.stepactions.dirichlet), which NED enforces.

The contact

contact = ParticlePenaltyFacetContactExplicit(
    "contact", model, particlesNearTheImpact, facets, penaltyParameter, searchDistance, journal,
    interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=True)
model.constraints["contact"] = contact

The contact points are the vertices of the given particles (only those the projectile can reach: every point is searched in every increment); the master is the faceted surface. The constraint pairs a point with a facet while the point approaches, pushes it back out through that facet once it has entered, and lets it slide only onto the facets next to it; a point found inside without having been seen outside gets no force and is reported. The force on the particles and the reaction on the projectile’s nodes are equal and opposite. See the class documentation for the gap, the pairing and what the constraint reports.

  • The penalty is a force per unit penetration of one contact point. It is a stiffness the critical time step does not see: keep \(2\sqrt{m/k}\), with \(m\) the mass a contact point carries, well above the time increment.

  • The search distance must exceed the distance a point travels towards the surface in one increment; a point in contact is never lost to it. A warning in the journal says when points enter deeper than half of it.

The time increment

NED takes the smaller of the critical time step of the elements (their own eigenvalue bound) and the bound of the stable time increment of the particles, bounded from their stiffness without assembling it, and scales it by courant-number. The bound of the particles is computed anew whenever the connectivity changes (see Choosing a solver, “Explicit dynamics”), which here is nearly every increment. It takes the tangent of the gradient-enhanced particles as it is, unsymmetric: while the concrete under the nose is crushed, GM7’s stress depends on the nonlocal strain through the steep compressive boundary, far more strongly than the nonlocal strain depends on the strain, and the bound follows the geometric mean of the two couplings, as the eigenvalues do. Here the limit of the particles is that of the nonlocal field, set by its micro-inertia, and it stays above the projectile’s elements: the elements set the time increment. Neither bound sees the penalty stiffness of the contact or of the supports; check them by hand, and check the result by halving the time increment.

Running it

step = AdaptiveStepForExplicitSimulations(
    0, model, fieldOutputController, journal, jobInfo,
    NEDParallel(jobInfo, journal, **{"output-frequency": 1}),
    outputManagers, stepActions, stepLength=500e-6, startInc=1.0, maxInc=1.0, minInc=1e-12, maxNumInc=10**7)
step.solve(resumeCheckpoint)

With output-frequency 1 the output managers see every increment: the run records the contact forces of both sides, the momentum and kinetic energy of the projectile and the total kinetic energy in every increment. A restart output manager writes checkpoints, and the run resumes from one (ResumeCheckpoint), so a long run survives an interruption. Nothing erodes failed concrete, so the run must stop before the slab engulfs the projectile: then a slab particle ends up on the far side of a facet it never approached, and the contact can no longer tell inside from outside. An output manager that raises ConditionalStop ends the step there.

What to check

A penalty contact in an explicit analysis can look healthy and be wrong: a time increment above the stability limit of the contact springs, a penalty that decides the answer, or contact points that tunnel through the search distance all produce plausible-looking histories. Record, in every increment, what the contact constraint reports and the projectile’s motion, and check:

  • Energy. Nothing does work on the model (the supports and the symmetry planes hold still), so the total kinetic energy must never exceed the projectile’s initial kinetic energy. A rebound is judged by its energy, not by the sign of the velocity: a penalty spring can push the projectile out only with energy the projectile brought in.

  • Momentum. contactForceOnParticles + contactForceOnMaster vanishes to round-off in every increment, and the projectile’s momentum (its nodal masses times the nodal velocities) changes by the accumulated impulse of contactForceOnMaster, the force of an increment times the mean of the increments before and after it (NED’s leapfrog), along the axis free of supports, to round-off.

  • Time increment. Halving courant-number must change the penetration history by much less than the penalty does.

  • Penalty. Over a tenfold range of the penalty, the penetration at a matched time may change by a few percent only, about the change of the overlap of the contact points. The force of the first touch is the penalty’s (the flat nose meets a few points at full speed); its average over a few microseconds is not.

  • Search distance. No point may be found inside (numberOfPointsFoundInside stays zero), and the deepest overlap (deepestPenetration) stays far below the search distance.

  • Symmetry. The quarter model reproduces the whole one: compare a small slab of the same particle size whole and quartered. The whole model is not exactly symmetric itself: the facets split every face along a fixed diagonal.

Done on a small slab of the same particle size, the checks came out as follows: the kinetic energy never exceeded the initial one; action and reaction cancelled to round-off in every increment, and the projectile’s momentum followed the contact impulse to round-off; halving the time increment changed the penetration by a few hundredths of a millimetre, a tenfold penalty range by about half a millimetre, the size of the change of the overlap; the deepest overlap was about a tenth of the search distance and no point was found inside; the quarter model followed the whole one to a few tenths of a millimetre. A run resumed from a checkpoint after an interruption followed the uninterrupted one.

One finding matters more than any of these: the response depends on the particle size more than on the time increment, the penalty or the symmetry. With GM7’s internal length well below the particle size, the softening concrete is resolved by the particles, not by the nonlocal strain.

What the run shows

The quarter model (3087 particles) ran until the nose reached the stop depth of 40 mm at 333 us, with the time increment set by the projectile’s elements throughout. The checks held over the whole run: the kinetic energy never exceeded the initial one, action and reaction cancelled to round-off, the projectile’s momentum followed the contact impulse to round-off, and no point was found inside. The projectile slowed from 300 to 148 m/s in the first 100 us and to 57 m/s at the stop; the contact force averaged over 5 us peaked at 3.3 MN after 20 to 25 us (the first touch, a few points at full speed, is a spike set by the penalty). At the stop, about two fifths of the particles had cracked, through the whole thickness and out to the supported edges, and the most crushed ones were under the nose and in the rear layer. The result depends on the particle size (see above), which the regularization of GM7 does not remove at this size.
