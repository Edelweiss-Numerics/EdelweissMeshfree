Constraints

The residual and the tangent

A constraint of EdelweissMeshfree is an EdelweissFE constraint, and enters the discrete system by EdelweissFE’s rule (see the EdelweissFE solver documentation, The residual and the tangent): its computeKernels(U_np, dU, P, K, timeStep) adds the residual contribution \(\mathbf{P}\) – the negative of the force the constraint exerts on its nodes, what an element of the same mechanics would add as its internal force – and its tangent \(\mathbf{K} = \partial \mathbf{P} / \partial \mathbf{U}\). A penalty constraint that pulls the displacement \(u = \mathbf{N} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U}\) of a particle towards \(\bar{u}\) adds

\[\mathbf{P} \mathrel{+}= k\, \mathbf{N}\, (\mathbf{N} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U} - \bar{u}), \qquad \mathbf{K} \mathrel{+}= k\, \mathbf{N} \otimes \mathbf{N} .\]

The nodal values of the particles and of the cells are increments on the current configuration, so these constraints read the increment dU. Their nodes are those the particles or material points currently interpolate from: a constraint finds them anew at the start of every increment, in updateConnectivity, which may also find the constraint out of reach (withinReach), e.g. a contact constraint whose particle is far from the boundary. A constraint takes part in the discrete system (active) while it is within reach, unless it is switched off from outside: setting active to False, e.g. by a modelupdate step action, switches a constraint off whatever it finds in reach, until it is switched on again. Switched off from outside, it rests: the model does not locate it anew, and it keeps what it found and computed last, its reaction force included. These are the rules of EdelweissFE’s constraint base class, see the EdelweissFE constraint documentation.

A constraint with Lagrange multipliers adds them as scalar variables of the model. The condition \(g\) it enforces is one on increments, like the nodal values; the multiplier \(\lambda\) is the force or traction that balances the total stresses of the particles or elements, so it is a total. A multiplier that prescribes the increment \(\Delta\bar{u}\) of a particle’s displacement \(u = \mathbf{N} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U}\) adds

\[\mathbf{P} \mathrel{+}= \lambda\, \mathbf{N}, \qquad P_{\lambda} \mathrel{+}= \mathbf{N} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U} - \Delta\bar{u}, \qquad \mathbf{K}_{U\lambda} \mathrel{+}= \mathbf{N}, \qquad \mathbf{K}_{\lambda U} \mathrel{+}= \mathbf{N}^T,\]

with \(\lambda\) read from U_np: the solver continues it from the last converged increment, also across a rebuilt discrete system, as the model keeps the converged multipliers in its scalar variables, and its checkpoint keeps them for a restart. The residual is linear in \(\lambda\), so where Newton’s method starts \(\lambda\) changes only its first residual, not the iterates after the first correction. The deprecated solvers (edelweissmeshfree.solvers.deprecated) continue the multipliers only while the discrete system is kept, restart them at zero after a rebuild, and do not check the residual of the multipliers for convergence.

Because \(\mathbf{K}\) is the derivative of exactly what is added to \(\mathbf{P}\), each constraint is checked by central differences of its own computeKernels in edelweissmeshfree/constraints/test/residual_tangent_test.py, with EdelweissFE’s check (edelweissfe.utils.residualtangentcheck); add a case there for a new one.

A constraint written for explicit analyses alone, with forces and without a tangent, derives from MPMExplicitConstraintBase and implements computeKernelsExplicit(U_np, dU, P, timeStep), with the same sign, and addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessTimes(U_np, dU, weights, bound, timeStep, nextIncrement=None), the bound of its stiffness for the critical time step of NED (from its springs, or nothing, saying so), with nextIncrement over the states the next increment can reach (follow-critical-time-step; the springs at the smallest gap of each point within it): an implicit solver refuses it. The explicit solver, in turn, accepts only a constraint that acts in an explicit analysis (actsInExplicitAnalysis): one that measures its condition at the particles’ current positions, as the penalty contacts do, explicit or not, and as the penalty weak Dirichlet condition does where it holds the displacement at zero (a support). A constraint on the increment, such as a weak Dirichlet condition that prescribes a change, would act as if nothing had moved there.

Points of a particle. A particle knows every field only by its value and its gradient at its center, so its own interpolation at a point of its own – a vertex, a face center, its center – is its shape functions linearized about the center, \(\mathbf{G}\) (getInterpolationVectorLinearizedAboutCenter()): for the displacement, the motion of the point. The shape functions at the point, \(\mathbf{N}\) (getInterpolationVector()), are exact at an arbitrary location and differ from \(\mathbf{G}\) away from the center. The constraints that act on points of particles (the penalty weak Dirichlet condition, the cartesian boundaries, the rigid body contacts, implicit and explicit) take one option, interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter (interpolationVectorAtPointOf()):

  • False, the default: they measure the field at the point and distribute their force there with \(\mathbf{N}\). In an explicit analysis, where the point is where the particle has moved it with \(\mathbf{G}\), the penalty stiffness \(k\,\mathbf{N}\mathbf{G}^\mathsf{T}\) is unsymmetric: it does work over a closed cycle of motion and can feed energy into softened material next to a support.

  • True: they use \(\mathbf{G}\) for both, for every field: the stiffness \(k\,\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^\mathsf{T}\) is symmetric and the constraint conservative; edelweissmeshfree/constraints/test/vertexpenaltywork_test.py checks that a support on the vertices then does no work over a closed cycle, nor does a contact on a face of a real particle, whose contact points always use \(\mathbf{G}\). The FE-particle impact (FE projectile on an RKPM slab) opts in for its supports. The two operations such a constraint needs besides its own physics – locating the nodes of its particles in its block, and distributing a force on a point – are in edelweissmeshfree.constraints.base.pointsofparticles, which the explicit constraints and the support use, with the penalty springs on points of a particle that give a constraint’s force and the bound of its stiffness for the critical time step of NED from the same code. A penalty contact of points of one particle with an obstacle in an explicit analysis derives from PenaltySpringsOnPointsOfOneParticleExplicit, as the Cartesian boundary and the contact with an analytical surface do: the base keeps the points, the nodes and the reach current, so a new one is the gap of a point, its gradient and whether the obstacle is within reach, and a constructor that calls the base’s.

The contact points on the faces of particles (the contact, the tie and the rigid contact of particle faces) have no such option: they always use \(\mathbf{G}\), since a point on a face stays on it only if it moves as the particle moves the face.

The base classes

Constraints are used to enforce certain conditions on the system. They are used to enforce boundary conditions, contact conditions, etc. Technically, a constraint is an entity of the discrete system like an element: it adds a residual contribution and its tangent, and it may introduce additional scalar variables (e.g., Lagrange multipliers) to enforce its condition. Unlike elements, constraints are not subject to external loads.

class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.base.mpmconstraintbase.MPMConstraintBase(name, model, *args, **kwargs)[source]

The base class of the constraints of EdelweissMeshfree; implement a new constraint by deriving from it.

A meshfree constraint is an EdelweissFE constraint (ConstraintBase), and follows its rules:

  • computeKernels() (U_np, dU, P, K, timeStep) adds the constraint’s residual contribution to P – the negative of the force it exerts on its nodes, what an element of the same mechanics would add as its internal force – and its tangent K = dP/dU. See the EdelweissFE solver documentation, The residual and the tangent.

  • The nodes of a meshfree constraint are those its particles or material points currently interpolate from, so they change as the kernel functions and cells move: updateConnectivity finds them anew at the start of every increment, and reports whether they changed.

  • A constraint may switch itself off in updateConnectivity, e.g. a contact constraint whose particle is far from the boundary: it sets withinReach; the model sees the change of active itself. A constraint switched off from outside, through active, stays off whatever it finds, and rests: the model does not locate it anew.

  • Every constraint declares the state it carries from one increment to the next, in checkpointedState (empty if it carries none), so that a restart can restore it.

The nodal values of the particles and of the cells are increments on the current configuration, so the constraints here read them from dU, the increment since the last converged state. A Lagrange multiplier, in contrast, is a force or a traction, which balances the total stresses of the particles or elements: it is a total value, read from U_np, where the solver continues it from the last converged increment. The model holds the multipliers as its scalar variables, and its checkpoint holds them with it.

A constraint that acts only in an explicit analysis, with forces and without a tangent, derives from MPMExplicitConstraintBase instead. Whether a constraint acts in an explicit analysis at all, it declares in actsInExplicitAnalysis.

The two operations every constraint on points of particles needs besides its own physics: where the nodes of its particles sit in its block, and how a force on a point of a particle reaches those nodes.

A constraint that measures a point of a particle – a vertex held by a support, a vertex in contact – acts on the nodes of the particle’s kernel functions, which change as the particle moves. Its block holds each of these nodes once (locateTheNodesOfParticles()). A force on the point reaches the nodes with the vector the constraint chose for the point (addForcesOnPointsOfParticle(); interpolationVectorAtPointOf()): the shape functions at the point, or the particle’s interpolation linearized about its center, with which it moves the point, the transpose of the point’s motion, so that a penalty on the point has a symmetric stiffness and does no work over a closed cycle. The explicit solver bounds that stiffness for its critical time step (addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessOfPointsOfParticle(), the one bound of penalty springs of EdelweissFE, edelweissfe.constraints.base.penaltysprings). A penalty constraint gives its springs on the points of a particle as SpringsOnPointsOfParticle, from one method for its force and its bound, so that the two cannot drift apart. A contact of points of one particle with an obstacle in an explicit analysis derives from PenaltySpringsOnPointsOfOneParticleExplicit, which owns the force, the bound and the gaps over the next increment: a new one is then its physics – the gap and its gradient – and a constructor. The support ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet gives its springs per particle the same way.

class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.base.pointsofparticles.SpringsOnPointsOfParticle(interpolations, directions, forces, stiffness)[source]

Penalty springs on points of one particle. Spring \(p\) measures a gap \(g_p\) of its point, positive where a contact is open, and acts along the unit direction \(\boldsymbol{n}_p\) of the gradient of the gap (for a contact the outward normal of the obstacle, for a support the axis it holds). Its law gives the force \(f_p = -k_p\, g_p\) on the point along \(\boldsymbol{n}_p\), which pushes a closed point out (\(g_p < 0\), \(f_p > 0\)) and pulls a held point back to where it is held, and the stiffness \(k_p = -\mathrm{d}f_p/\mathrm{d}g_p > 0\). The force \(f_p \boldsymbol{n}_p\) reaches the nodes with the vector of the point (addForcesOnPointsOfParticle()), and the stiffness is \(k_p\, \boldsymbol{a}_p \otimes \boldsymbol{a}_p\) with \(\boldsymbol{a}_p = \boldsymbol{I}_p \otimes \boldsymbol{n}_p\) (addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessOfPointsOfParticle()). The bound takes the vector of the point for its motion as well, which it is where the constraint uses the particle’s interpolation linearized about its center, or at the center; elsewhere the point moves with that interpolation, not with the shape functions the force is distributed with (interpolationVectorAtPointOf()).

computeKernels(U_np, dU, P, K, timeStep)[source]

Add this constraint’s contribution P to the residual, and its tangent K = dP/dU.

A constraint follows the rule every element and every load follows (see The residual and the tangent): P is the force the constraint needs at its degrees of freedom, i.e. the negative of the force it exerts on the nodes, exactly as an element of the same mechanics would add its internal force. A constraint that holds a degree of freedom \(u\) at \(\bar{u}\) with the stiffness \(k\), for example, adds

\[P \mathrel{+}= k\,(u - \bar{u}), \qquad K \mathrel{+}= k .\]

A Lagrange multiplier constraint \(g(\boldsymbol{u}) = 0\) adds \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \partial g / \partial \boldsymbol{u}\) to the rows of its nodes and \(g\) to the rows of its multipliers.

Both P and K are augmented, never overwritten. K must be the derivative of what is added to P: a test can then check it by finite differences, which also catches a P whose sign alone is wrong. A P and a K negated together pass that check, so a test checks the direction of the forces the constraint exerts as well.

Parameters:

  • U_np (ndarray) – The current solution, restricted to this constraint’s degrees of freedom; its own scalar variables (e.g. Lagrange multipliers) come last.

  • dU (ndarray) – The solution increment since the last converged state, likewise restricted.

  • P (ndarray) – The residual contribution to augment.

class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.particlepenaltyweakdirichtlet.ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet(name, model, constrainedParticles, field, prescribedStepDelta, penaltyParameter, constrain='center', interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=False, **kwargs)[source]

This is an implementation of weak Dirichlet boundary conditions using a penalty formulation. It constrains a material point field increment.

In an implicit analysis, it penalizes the difference between the increment of the field at the constrained points, \(\boldsymbol{N} \cdot \Delta\boldsymbol{U}\), and the prescribed increment, the change of prescribedStepDelta times f_t over the increment. The same vector measures the increment and distributes the force, so the stiffness is symmetric: the shape functions at the point, \(\boldsymbol{N}\) (the default), or, with interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter, the particle’s interpolation linearized about its center, \(\boldsymbol{G}\), its own view of the point.

In an explicit analysis, the nodes of the kernel functions carry no increment to constrain: the particles have moved when the constraints are evaluated. There the constraint holds displacement components at zero, as a support, e.g. on a symmetry plane or at a clamped edge, and it measures them where the constrained points are now: the force on a constrained point is

\[f_i = -k \left( x_i - X_i \right)\]

for every constrained component \(i\), with \(\boldsymbol{X}\) the position of the point when the constraint was created. The point (the center or a vertex of the particle) is where the particle has put it: it moves with the particle, in an increment by \(\boldsymbol{G} \cdot \Delta\boldsymbol{U}\). By default the force is distributed over the nodes of the kernel functions with the shape functions at the point, \(\boldsymbol{N}\), which away from the center is not \(\boldsymbol{G}\): the penalty stiffness \(k\,\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{G}^\mathsf{T}\) is unsymmetric, does work over a closed cycle of motion, and can feed energy into the material next to the support once that has softened. With interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter it is distributed with \(\boldsymbol{G}\), the transpose of the point’s motion: the stiffness \(k\,\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{G}^\mathsf{T}\) is symmetric and the support conservative (interpolationVectorAtPointOf()). The force acts on both sides of \(X_i\). It acts in an explicit analysis (actsInExplicitAnalysis) only if it constrains the displacement and every prescribed change is zero: an explicit analysis prescribes motion through velocities, not through penalized displacements.

A support holds only as well as its penalty is stiff against the material next to it: a penalty that is merely comparable to the stiffness of a particle lets the support give way and spring back, an error that does not shrink with the particle size. A stiffer one costs time increments: the explicit solver adds its springs to the frequencies of the nodes they hold (addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessTimes()), and a support far stiffer than the particles sets the critical time step, about \(2 \sqrt{m / k}\) with \(m\) the mass a constrained point carries; see example 186.

Constrains the field of two particles to change equally, in some components, with Lagrange multipliers.

Each particle interpolates the field at its center from the nodes of its kernel functions, as an increment on the current configuration: \(\boldsymbol{N}_m \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U}\) for the master particle, \(\boldsymbol{N}_s \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U}\) for the slave particle. class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.particlelagrangianweakdirichlet.ParticleLagrangianWeakDirichlet(name, constrainedParticle, field, prescribedStepDelta, model, location='center', faceID=None, vertexID=None)[source]

Prescribes the increment of a field at one point of one particle – its center, the center of one of its faces, or one of its vertices – with Lagrange multipliers.

It is the Lagrange multiplier counterpart of ParticlePenaltyWeakDirichlet. A penalty spring enforces the prescribed value only approximately, the more closely the stiffer it is; a multiplier enforces it exactly, at the cost of one more unknown per prescribed component, and of a zero block on the diagonal of the tangent. To constrain a set of particles, create one constraint per particle with ParticleLagrangianWeakDirichletOnParticleSetFactory(): every constraint couples only the nodes of its own particle, so its tangent block stays small, where one constraint on all particles would couple all nodes of the set in one dense block.

A particle interpolates the field from the nodes of its kernel functions, as an increment on the current configuration: \(u(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}) \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U}\). At the constrained point \(\boldsymbol{x}_c\) and for every prescribed component \(i\), a multiplier \(\lambda_i\) enforces

\[g_i = \boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_c) \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U}_i - \Delta\bar{u}_i = 0,\]

\[\mathbf{P}_{U_i} \mathrel{+}= \lambda_i\, \boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_c), \qquad P_{\lambda_i} \mathrel{+}= g_i, \qquad \mathbf{K}_{U_i \lambda_i} \mathrel{+}= \boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_c), \qquad \mathbf{K}_{\lambda_i U_i} \mathrel{+}= \boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_c)^T,\]

so that it acts on the particle with the point force \(-\lambda_i\) at \(\boldsymbol{x}_c\). The tangent is symmetric, as the constraint stems from the potential \(\sum_i \lambda_i g_i\).

class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.particlelagrangiancartesianboundary.ParticleLagrangianContactCartesianBoundaryConstraint(name, particle, component, boundaryPosition, model, location='center', faceID=None, vertexID=None, velocity=0.0, interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=False)[source]

This class implements a Lagrangian contact boundary constraint. It prevents a particle from penetrating a defined boundary surface. The constraint is applied using Lagrange multipliers to enforce the contact condition. Only cartesian planes are supported as boundary surfaces.

The constraint equation is defined as

\[g = \boldsymbol{n} \cdot (\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{boundary}}) \leq 0,\]

where \(\boldsymbol{n}\) is the orientation of the boundary, \(\boldsymbol{x}\) the particle position, and \(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{boundary}}\) the boundary position.

The orientation is determined based on the initial position of the particle relative to the boundary.

The particle interpolates its displacement from the nodes of its kernel functions, as an increment on the current configuration, so the gap is measured with the increment \(\boldsymbol{I} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{U}_j\) of the constrained component \(j\) from the position at the start of the increment, where \(\boldsymbol{I}\) is the shape functions at the point, \(\boldsymbol{N}\) (the default), or, with interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter, the particle’s interpolation linearized about its center, \(\boldsymbol{G}\), with which it moves the point (interpolationVectorAtPointOf()). While the particle is in contact, a multiplier \(\lambda\) enforces \(g = 0\), and the constraint adds

\[\mathbf{P}_{U_j} \mathrel{+}= \lambda\, \frac{\partial g}{\partial \Delta\mathbf{U}_j}, \qquad P_{\lambda} \mathrel{+}= g, \qquad \mathbf{K}_{U_j \lambda} \mathrel{+}= \frac{\partial g}{\partial \Delta\mathbf{U}_j}, \qquad \mathbf{K}_{\lambda U_j} \mathrel{+}= \frac{\partial g}{\partial \Delta\mathbf{U}_j}^T,\]

with \(\partial g / \partial \Delta\mathbf{U}_j = \boldsymbol{n}_j \boldsymbol{I}\). The multiplier is the contact force, positive in compression. A particle that does not penetrate the boundary, or whose multiplier would pull it towards the boundary, is out of contact: the constraint then adds \(P_{\lambda} \mathrel{+}= \lambda\) and \(K_{\lambda\lambda} \mathrel{+}= 1\), which holds the multiplier at zero.

The gap is one on increments, the multiplier is a total: the contact force that balances the total stress of the particle, read from U_np, where it continues from the last converged increment. reactionForce holds it.

class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.particlemortarweakdirichlet.ParticleMortarWeakDirichlet(name, constrainedParticles, field, prescribedStepDelta, model, multiplierOrder=4)[source]

Mortar-type weak Dirichlet boundary condition on a set of particles.

Instead of collocating the constraint pointwise at every particle (which produces alternating point reactions on nearly incompressible media and, with it, a pressure checkerboard along the boundary), the constraint is enforced in the integrated sense

g_k = sum_q w_q P_k(xi_q) ( u_i(x_q) - ubar_i ) = 0, k = 0..multiplierOrder,

class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.particlefiniteelementmortartie.ParticleFiniteElementMortarTie(name, model, interfaceParticles, finiteElementInterfaceNodes, quadraturePointsPerEdge=8)[source]

Ties an RKPM domain to a finite element domain along a common interface, in two dimensions: the displacement of the particles and the displacement of the finite elements agree on the interface in the weak (mortar) sense.

What is tied. On the interface \(\Gamma\), the finite element side interpolates the displacement with the shape functions \(N_a\) of its quadratic element edges, the RKPM side with the reproducing kernel shape functions \(\Psi_K\) of the particles there. The two do not match pointwise: the reproducing kernel shape functions are smooth and not interpolating, the element edges are piecewise polynomials. The tie therefore asks that their difference be orthogonal to the multiplier space – the shape functions of the element edges, one multiplier per interface node and displacement component:

\[g_{ai} = \int_\Gamma N_a \left( \sum_b N_b\, \Delta U_{bi} - \sum_K \Psi_K\, \Delta U_{Ki} \right) d\Gamma = 0.\]

Incremental, on the current configuration. RKPM is updated-Lagrangian: its nodal values are the coefficients of one increment, on the configuration at the start of the increment, where the kernel functions sit with their particles. The tie therefore couples increments \(\Delta U\), and integrates on that configuration: the element edges are placed at their nodes’ converged positions, \(\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{X} + \boldsymbol{U}\). Both sides start stress-free and tied, so the increments add up to a tied total displacement. During an increment the configuration is fixed, so \(g\) is linear in \(\Delta U\), and its derivatives

\[\frac{\partial g_{ai}}{\partial \Delta U_{bi}} = \int_\Gamma N_a N_b \, d\Gamma, \qquad \frac{\partial g_{ai}}{\partial \Delta U_{Ki}} = -\int_\Gamma N_a \Psi_K \, d\Gamma\]

are computed once per increment, in updateConnectivity().

Forces. The multiplier \(\lambda_{ai}\) is the interface traction, interpolated like the displacement of the element edges. Unlike the increments it couples, it is a total quantity: the stresses on both sides are total, so is the force that the interface transmits between them. Following the rule of every constraint (see MPMConstraintBase), the tie adds

\[P_{bi} \mathrel{+}= \sum_a \lambda_{ai} \frac{\partial g_{ai}}{\partial \Delta U_{bi}}, \qquad P_{Ki} \mathrel{+}= \sum_a \lambda_{ai} \frac{\partial g_{ai}}{\partial \Delta U_{Ki}}, \qquad P_{\lambda_{ai}} \mathrel{+}= g_{ai},\]

and the tangent, symmetric as the tie stems from the potential \(\sum_{ai} \lambda_{ai} g_{ai}\). The finite element side receives the force \(-\int_\Gamma N_b \lambda_i \, d\Gamma\), the RKPM side the opposite one.

The integral. Gauss quadrature on every element edge, in the configuration at the start of the increment. The reproducing kernel shape functions at a quadrature point are those of the interface particle the point lies on. A field that is linear in space is represented exactly on both sides, so it is tied exactly, whatever the quadrature (the patch test).

Rules for the interface.

  • The finite element side carries the multipliers. Its nodes must not be prescribed by a Dirichlet boundary condition as well, which would constrain them twice.

  • The finite element side is given by its interface nodes, in order along the interface: corner node, midside node, corner node, midside node, …, corner node – the edges of quadratic elements. The tie checks that the order is one along the interface: every midside node lies near the middle of its corners (isQuadraticEdge()), and the interface does not turn back at a corner node (turnsBack()). A node set in the order of its labels, for instance, is refused.

  • The RKPM side is given by its particles along the interface.

Contact of particles with finite elements

Particles meet a body of finite elements through EdelweissFE’s contact constraint, edelweissfe.constraints.surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty, the same one that couples two bodies of finite elements: it is one contact for every kind of contact point (see EdelweissFE’s contact theory, Contact points: the slave side as points and One contact for every kind of contact point). The contact points of particles are a third kind, next to the nodes and the quadrature points of finite element faces: quadrature points on faces of particles – a surface of particles, as the generators and the side sets of an Exodus mesh make them, or (particle, face) pairs (ContactPointsOnParticleFaces). Each moves with the particle’s interpolation linearized about its center, \(\mathbf{G}\) (see Points of a particle above), its force reaches the nodes of the particle’s kernel functions with the same \(\mathbf{G}\), and it stands for the area of its share of the face, so the penalty is a stiffness per unit area, as for finite elements. The points measure their areas from the model when they are made, as the particles integrate: in 3D the area of the share; in a plane model a particle is a slice of unit thickness, so a point stands for its length times 1, and the finite elements the particles meet must be of plane sections of thickness 1 for the forces to balance (nothing checks it); in an axisymmetric model the area of its ring about the axis (Axisymmetric particles). The particles are updated-Lagrangian: the nodes of their kernel functions carry the increment, so the displacement a point moves with is the increment, from where the point was at the last update of the connectivity, its origin.

The master is the faceted boundary of the finite elements, which EdelweissFE’s surface element generator creates; it may have corners, convex and re-entrant, such as the crests and roots of a thread (EdelweissFE’s contact theory, Corners of the master: creases, the signed distance and the pseudonormal). An overlap at the start is either real, such as a groove cut smaller than the thread, and pushed apart, at once or over a time (initialOverlap=pushApart, the default), or an error of the geometry, and adjusted: kept as the stress-free state (initialOverlap=adjust; An initial overlap). The flat faces of particles that approximate a curved interface are the second kind: they are chords of its arcs and lie inside it by their sag, an error of the geometry (adjust). The contact acts in implicit and explicit analyses. In NED, the stiffness of its springs adds to the frequencies of the nodes they couple, on the particles’ side inside their field matrix (Choosing a solver), on the side of the finite elements as for any penalty constraint.

Its tests are edelweissmeshfree/constraints/test/particlefacecontact_test.py (the force of a pressed face, the tangent by central differences, pairing, entering, tunnelling, an overlap at the start ramped out or adjusted, restart) and examples 185 (the same result as an analytic wall), 187 (impact of particles and of finite elements on finite elements, and a stiff contact that sets the time increment), 191 (FE projectile on an RKPM slab), 192 (the contact patch test, implicit: the pressure a block of particles transmits between two platens of finite elements, against a block of finite elements; uniform away from the free ends of the particles, and why not at them, see the module of the contact points) and 193 (a toy pull-out: the head of a stud of finite elements bearing on particles, implicit).

The same constraint with the law type=tied – the input-file spelling; in Python the field of its options is contactType="tied" (type would shadow the builtin), the other options keep their names – ties particles to finite elements (EdelweissFE’s contact theory, A tie: contact that never opens and never slides; it is not EdelweissFE’s tie constraint, which eliminates the degrees of freedom of slave nodes, see contact-two-ties). At the first update, every point of a face of a particle within searchDistance of the master is paired once with the closest point of its closest facet, mapped onto the facet’s parent face, and held there in every direction by penalty springs, in tension and in compression. A gap or an overlap of the two surfaces at the pairing – two meshes of one curved surface never coincide – is part of the state at rest. The particles are updated-Lagrangian, as for the contact: at every update the tied points are located again, where the particles have moved them, and their displacement from rest is measured from there, so a point stays tied to the same point of the master however far it moves. The tie joins a region of particles to a far field of finite elements, in implicit and explicit analyses, where the mortar tie of Coupled RKPM + FE (implicit, 2D) does not reach. A new mesh of the elements hands the tie on (EdelweissFE’s contact theory, A tie: contact that never opens and never slides): the same points stay tied to the same points of the master, displaced from rest as at the last accepted state.

  • What the tie accepts in NED: the state of the evaluation after the particles are reconnected, the first at the accepted configuration. That state is what a checkpoint holds.

  • No new mesh during the analysis: NED does not implement model modifiers that act after the start of the analysis in a model with particles, and refuses them (NotImplementedError).

  • What a new mesh starts from: in a following step, or in a run resumed from the checkpoint, the accepted state above. The points of particles are never regenerated by a change of the mesh, so they need no interpolation from old points to new ones.

  • 194, the patch test of the tie, implicit: a column of particles between two blocks of elements, stretched and sheared, with a gap or an overlap at the start. It transmits the force of the column, and the error halves with the size of the particles; the traction at single points varies. The mechanism is that of example 192: the particles balance a homogeneous stress with their shape functions at the centers of their faces, the tie loads them with \(\mathbf{G}\) at 2 x 2 points per face, and the particles deviate slightly from the homogeneous state within two or three particles of a side of the column. The four springs of a face hold it against the tilts of that deviation, so the stiffer the tie, the more the traction varies across a face. The traction of a tie at a single point of particles is therefore not a measure of the stress there; its resultant over a few particles is. In shear, the springs that hold the sides of the particles add a smaller band and their compliance; the module says why.

  • 195, explicit: a pulse through a bar of elements with a tied segment, against the bar of elements alone. A segment of elements tied in reflects what the two springs of its ties reflect, transmits the rest, and delays the transmitted pulse by their compliance, to first order in how finely the elements resolve the pulse. A segment of particles changes the pulse more: it reflects more at its ends, and delays the pulse along its length, by a phase speed of its own. Both shrink with the size of the particles and elements, the reflection faster than the ties’ own. The state the ties accept in NED hands them on to a new mesh of the elements without a jump of their forces; the test makes that mesh by hand in the middle of the pulse, standing for a refinement in a following step or a resumed run.

The springs of the tie raise the frequencies of the points they hold, which NED’s critical time step counts, like the contact’s normal spring, along every axis (Choosing a solver).

Moving a deck from ParticlePenaltyFacetContactExplicit. The contact of the vertices of particles with a faceted surface is replaced by this contact, as in examples 185, 187 and 191:

  • Why. It was a second contact next to EdelweissFE’s, without its corners of the master, its overlap at the start, its implicit path and its rigid bodies; it acted on the vertices, with a penalty per point, in explicit analyses only, and its springs were not in the critical time step of NED. The contact above is one constraint for every kind of contact point, the faces of particles included.

  • The points. Contact points on the faces of the particles that can meet the surface, a surface of particles or (particle, face) pairs, instead of the particles whose vertices met it. The option interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter is gone: the points always move, and distribute their force, with \(\mathbf{G}\), which the option chose; its default, \(\mathbf{N}\), is not available.

  • The penalty, a stiffness per unit area. The old contact put its penalty \(k_v\) at every vertex of every given particle: \(n_v\) vertices on a face of the area \(A_f\) (2 in 2D, 4 in 3D), so \(p = k_v\, n_v / A_f\) per unit area keeps the stiffness of the face, whatever the number of contact points per face. Example 191: \(2 \times 10^5\) per vertex, faces of 20 mm x 20 mm, so \(2 \times 10^5 \cdot 4 / 400 = 2 \times 10^3\).

  • The master is a surface of finite elements of the model, which takes the reaction. A deck whose facets were moved by something else (twoWayCoupling=False, such as a fixed floor) holds the nodes of a layer of elements under the surface (example 185), meets a discrete rigid body (below), or, for a plane, uses the Cartesian boundary (ParticlePenaltyCartesianBoundaryConstraintExplicit).

  • The other arguments. searchDistance moves into the options of the contact; the contact searches every increment in a model with particles. entryDepthWarningFraction is gone: the contact reports a point that enters deeper than half the search distance. nQuadraturePoints is required: one point per face is the face’s center; take 2 per edge (2 in 2D, 4 in 3D) for a quadratic master or an axisymmetric model.

  • The outputs have EdelweissFE’s names: contactForceOnParticles is contactForceOnSlave, and numberOfPointsInContact is numberOfPointsWithASpring; penaltyEnergy, deepestPenetration, numberOfPointsFoundInside and contactForceOnMaster keep their names.

  • The time increment. The springs now enter NED’s critical time step: a stiff penalty lowers the time increment, where the old contact left it to the user to keep \(2\sqrt{m/k}\) above it (example 187 tests a stiff contact that sets the time increment). Choose the penalty as for a tie, \(k = \beta E / h\) (Coupled RKPM + FE).

Example 191 before and after (facets are the facets of the projectile; particles those it can reach; the penalty was per vertex, it is per unit area now; all six faces of every particle, also the inner ones, which the projectile meets as it penetrates):

# before
contact = ParticlePenaltyFacetContactExplicit(

    interpolationLinearizedAboutCenter=True,

# after

    "contact",
    model,
    ContactPointsOnParticleFaces(
        [(particle, faceID) for particle in particles for faceID in range(1, 7)], model, nQuadraturePoints=4

Contact of particles with a rigid body

The same contact points meet a discrete rigid body – a support, an indenter, a projectile that does not deform – through EdelweissFE’s edelweissfe.constraints.surfacetodiscreterigidbodypenalty, the contact of the faces and nodes of finite elements with a rigid body (EdelweissFE’s contact theory, Integrated contact against a discrete rigid body): the constraint is built with ContactPointsOnParticleFaces in place of the faces of elements. Each point is paired with the plane of its closest triangle of the rigid surface, which moves and rotates with the body’s reference point; the force of a point reaches the nodes of the kernel functions of its particle with \(\mathbf{G}\), and the reference point with the force and its moment. Points farther than the search distance from the rigid surface take no part until they approach. Its tests are edelweissmeshfree/constraints/test/particlefacerigidcontact_test.py (the force and the moment of a pressed face, action = reaction, the tangent by central differences against a moved and rotated body, a particle that approaches), on stand-ins of particles with given vertices and interpolation vectors, not on Marmot particles. The particle constraints for analytical surfaces above (ParticlePenaltyRigidBodyContact, with an implicit function) remain the way to describe a boundary by a formula rather than by a mesh.

The contact points of particles: quadrature points on the faces of particles at the boundary of a body, for the contact constraints of EdelweissFE, which pair them with a master surface of finite element faces (edelweissfe.constraints.surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty) or with a discrete rigid body (edelweissfe.constraints.surfacetodiscreterigidbodypenalty). They are a third kind of EdelweissFE’s contact points (edelweissfe.constraints.base.contactpoints), next to the nodes and the quadrature points of finite element faces: a point \(\boldsymbol{x}_q\) of a particle face, with \(c = G(\boldsymbol{x}_q)\), at \(\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{x}_q^n + G(\boldsymbol{x}_q) \cdot \Delta\boldsymbol{U}\), standing for the area of its share of the face (The area of a point, below). The contact with the law type=tied, a tie, takes them alike: it ties faces of particles to finite elements. A change of the mesh never regenerates them (regenerated()), so a tie hands its state on across a new mesh of the master only.

A face of a particle moves with its particle: the particle moves every point of its own, a vertex or a point on a face, by \(\Delta\boldsymbol{x} = \sum_B G_B(\boldsymbol{x}) \Delta\boldsymbol{U}_B\), with its shape functions linearized about its center (getInterpolationVectorLinearizedAboutCenter()), which are affine in \(\boldsymbol{x}\). So a face stays flat if it is flat, and a point at fixed parametric coordinates \(\xi_q\) of the face stays there. Such a point is a contact point: it sits at \(\boldsymbol{x}_q = \sum_a N_a(\xi_q) \boldsymbol{x}_a\) (the face’s vertices \(\boldsymbol{x}_a\)), and stands for the area of its share of the face, of the measure \(J_q w_q\). The contact measures its motion with \(G(\boldsymbol{x}_q)\), and its contact force reaches the nodes of the particle’s kernel functions with the same \(G\), the transpose of its motion: the penalty stiffness \(k\, G G^T\) is symmetric and the contact conservative. It is always \(G\), because a point at fixed \(\xi_q\) stays on the face only if it moves as the particle moves the face: with the shape functions at the point, \(N(\boldsymbol{x}_q)\), it would drift off the face within an increment and jump back, by \(\boldsymbol{n} \cdot (G - N) \Delta\boldsymbol{U}\), when the next update takes its origin from the face again.

The particles are updated-Lagrangian: the nodes of their kernel functions carry the increment \(\Delta\boldsymbol{U}\) since the last update of the connectivity, which a model with particles makes at the start of every increment, not a total displacement. So the displacement a point moves with is that increment: its origin \(\boldsymbol{x}_q^n\) is where it was at the update, and its displacement from there is \(c \cdot \Delta\boldsymbol{U}\) (ContactPointsOnParticleFaces.displacementsOfLocatedPoints()). The contact constraints pass the total displacement U and the increment dU alike, and these points take dU.

For a constant pressure on a face, \(\sum_q w_q J_q G(\boldsymbol{x}_q) = A\, G(\boldsymbol{x}_c)\) for every rule that integrates a linear function exactly: one point at the face’s center already delivers the resultant and its first moment, everything a particle with a homogeneous deformation can feel. Two points per edge (2 in 2D, 2 x 2 in 3D) integrate exactly as well the linearly varying gap of a tilted face, and the quadratic shape functions of a master face of the same size, with which the contact loads the master (edelweissfe.constraints.surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty).

The area of a point is the area of the body its share of the face stands for, as the particles integrate, measured from the model when the points are made, by the rule of every contact point (edelweissfe.constraints.base.contactpoints): its measure \(J_q w_q\) in the model’s own space – an area in 3D, a length in 2D – times the out-of-plane extent \(b\) of the body at the point, \(A_q = b\, J_q w_q\).

  • In 3D, \(b = 1\).

  • A plane model with particles is a slice of unit thickness, \(b = 1\): Marmot’s plane-strain particles integrate their forces and masses per unit thickness, and so do their points. The finite elements the particles meet must then be of plane sections of thickness 1 for the forces to balance; nothing checks this.

  • In an axisymmetric model, the section r-z of a body of revolution (axisymmetric), the particles are axisymmetric (prepareYourself() checks them), and a face of a particle is an edge of the section that sweeps a ring (a conical, cylindrical or plane annular strip) about the axis. A point stands for the area of its ring, \(b = 2 \pi r_q\), with the radius \(r_q\) of the point, so that the contact forces are those of the full rings, as the particles’ forces and masses are. A point on the axis stands for a ring of radius 0, no area: the axis is a line, not a surface of the body. A point below the axis by no more than the round-off of the diagonal of its particle’s bounding box (axisTolerance, the rule of Marmot’s axisymmetric particles and elements for their vertices) is on it; one further below is refused. The rule is EdelweissFE’s, for every kind of contact point (circumferenceAt()); the particles give it their diagonals.

The ring weight is linear in \(r\) along an edge, so one point at the edge’s center already gives the exact area of its ring, \(2 \pi \bar r L\). The nodal forces of a constant pressure on the ring, \(\int 2 \pi r\, G(\boldsymbol{x})\, \mathrm{d}s\), are quadratic along the edge: two points per edge integrate them exactly, one point does not.

The load at a free end. The particles balance a homogeneous stress with the traction on their faces distributed with their shape functions at the centers of the faces, \(N(\boldsymbol{x}_f)\), one point per face: their smoothed gradients integrate \(N\) over their faces with that rule, and a particle distributes its own pressure on a face so. Only that load keeps the homogeneous state. The contact distributes with \(G\) instead, alike at one or two points per face; the shape functions at the points, \(N(\boldsymbol{x}_q)\), at two points per face would differ from it as well, since \(N\) is not affine. On each face, the difference has no resultant and no moment, and it does not cancel between neighbouring particles, since \(G\) is not continuous from one particle to the next. Along a straight row of equal particles it repeats from one column of kernel nodes to the next, without resultant in each column, so it changes the pressure alike along the row. Where the row ends at a free corner of the body, the kernel functions are no longer translates of each other, and the pressure on the last two or three particles deviates from uniform by a few percent, by the same profile at every particle size. Two points per face, which a quadratic master needs, also hold the tilt of a face: at a free end the pressure varies within each face as well (example 192, the contact patch test).

class edelweissmeshfree.constraints.base.contactpointsonparticlefaces.ContactPointsOnParticleFaces(faces, model, nQuadraturePoints)[source]

The quadrature points of faces of particles, used as contact points.

The points are numbered face after face, in the order the faces are given, and within a face by quadrature point. The area each point stands for is measured when the points are made, from the model, in the configuration of the particles then, the reference of the contact: its measure times the out-of-plane extent of the body at it (see The area of a point in the module documentation). A penalty per unit area times it is the constant stiffness of the point’s spring.

A located point takes the nodes of its particle’s kernel functions into the block of the constraint; a node shared by several located points, of one particle or of neighbouring ones, is located once (locateTheNodesOfParticles()). The interpolation vectors of the located points form one sparse matrix \(C\) over these nodes, made once per update of the connectivity: the points move by \(C\, \Delta\boldsymbol{U}\) from their origins, where they were at the update, and their forces reach the nodes with \(C^T\). The vectors are \(G(\boldsymbol{x}_q)\), the particle’s interpolation linearized about its center.

Parameters:

  • faces (dict | Iterable) – The faces: a surface of particles (face ID -> particles, e.g. from the generators of EdelweissMeshfree or from the side sets of an Exodus mesh), or (particle, face ID) pairs. The face IDs follow the Abaqus numbering, from 1 (verticesOfFace); others are refused.

  • model (FEModel) – The model of the particles, whose kind – 3D, plane or axisymmetric – the areas are measured as.

  • nQuadraturePoints (int) – The number of Gauss points per face: \(n\) on an edge (2D), \(n \times n\) on a face (3D). One point, the face’s center, loads a quadratic master face only at its midside node and does not integrate the ring weight of an axisymmetric model: take 2 per edge (2 in 2D, 4 in 3D) for a quadratic master or an axisymmetric model (see the module documentation).
